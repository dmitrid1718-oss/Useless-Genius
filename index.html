ДОБРО ПОЖАЛОВАТЬ / FROM THE ARCHIVE DESK

Welcome to Useless Genius

History remembers the winners. This place is for everyone else.

Here you’ll find brilliant inventions that were shelved, ignored, stolen, banned or simply born too early: a steam engine that ran a week after its inventor died, a computer that ran on water, a Moon plan drawn up by a man hiding under a false name. Many of the stories come from the Soviet Union, where world-class ideas often ended up in a drawer. Others come from anywhere a good idea met the wrong moment.

None of it was actually useless. The world just wasn’t ready.

Pick a drawer and start digging.

ЛИЧНОЕ ДЕЛО / A NOTE FROM THE FOUNDER

Five Miles From Buran

Why I started Useless Genius, and what I hope comes next

“Buran ended up in a hangar. I ended up on a plane. We were both built for a future that disappeared. The difference is that I got to keep going.”

Read D’s story ЧИТАТЬ ИСТОРИЮ

The morning I was there

On November 15, 1988, the Soviet Union launched its space shuttle, Buran, from Baikonur. I was a young soldier then. My unit was stationed about five miles from the launch pad. We weren't there to watch the show. We were there as backup, in case it blew up.

It didn't. The giant Energia rocket carried Buran into orbit. A little over three hours later, Buran came back and landed itself on a runway, through a strong crosswind, with nobody on board. No pilot. Just the machine and the work of the thousands of engineers who built it. It was a remarkable test flight.

And then Buran never flew again.

A country that ended

Not long after, the country I had served was gone. When the Soviet Union collapsed in 1991, I was 21, and that same year I was on a plane to America to start from zero.

Buran ended up in a hangar. I ended up on a plane. We were both built for a future that disappeared. The difference is that I got to keep going.

A life with tools

In America I built my life with my hands and my head. I worked as an auto mechanic and in metal fabrication. Across overlapping roles, I spent 25 years in mechanical engineering, 20 years in textile engineering and 15 years in product development, and I have used SolidWorks for 25 years. I still keep sewing machines at home. I have always been the kind of person who wants to know how a thing works, and then how to make it better.

Why I started Useless Genius

Recently I started digging into the stories behind the machines and discoveries I grew up around, and I kept finding the same story again and again.

Brilliant people did their best work in prison design bureaus, in exile, in besieged cities, or while being told they were wrong. Tupolev designed bombers behind bars. Korolev survived the gold mines of Kolyma, then launched Sputnik while his name was kept a state secret. Kuznetsov's Moon rocket engines survived in storage after the program was cancelled, and decades later modified versions flew on American rockets. Gleb Lozino-Lozinsky led the development of the shuttle I stood guard over. Many of these people never got a thank-you. Some never got their names on their own work.

Useless Genius is my thank-you. It's a memory wall, so future generations can see who these people were and what they built. The name is a joke with a point: none of these ideas were useless. The world just wasn't ready for them, or the system didn't know what to do with them.

Back From the Drawer

The more I read, the more I'm convinced that some of these ideas aren't finished.

They're already coming back. The phage-therapy tradition preserved in Tbilisi is drawing renewed interest as antibiotic resistance grows; phages have helped some patients with difficult infections, although treatment remains investigational in the United States. Ground-effect craft like the old ekranoplans are being developed again for coastal transport. Ternary computing is being studied again for low-power electronics. Shukhov's lattice towers still teach architects how to do more with less steel. The drawer is full of tested ideas, and some of them may fit today's problems better than they fit their own time.

And there is a treasure even bigger than the ideas: the people. Engineers trained in the Soviet system are now in their 60s, 70s and 80s, living in Russia, Ukraine, Georgia, Israel, Germany, America and everywhere else. Many of them carry knowledge of projects that were cancelled, classified or simply never written down. When they're gone, that knowledge goes with them.

So here is my idea. Bring them back to the workbench. Record their stories. Open their drawers. Pair the old hands with young builders who have 3D printers, CAD software and cheap electronics, and pick one shelved idea at a time to give it a second life, in the open, for everyone to see.

This isn't about politics or flags. It's about the engineering heritage of brilliant people from every corner of the former Soviet Union, many of whom were punished by the very system they served. I want a comeback, a big one, but in the good way: ideas that heal, carry, light, connect and help people live better.

An invitation

If you are one of those engineers, or the son or daughter of one, I want to hear your story. If you're young and you love to build things, come too. The best ideas happen when a person who designed something in 1982 meets a person who can prototype it this weekend.

Five miles from Buran, I learned that a machine can do the impossible when enough good minds put their work into it. I'd like to see that again.

The drawer is open.

— D, founder of Useless Genius

UG / 001 · COMPUTING1958

Setun

СЕТУНЬ · ТРОИЧНАЯ ЭВМ

Inventor:
Nikolai Brusentsov & team
Location:
Moscow State University
Filed under:
Ternary computing / ferrite logic

−1, 0, +1.
Another way to count.

Read story ЧИТАТЬ

In 1958, a small team at Moscow State University built a computer that counted differently. Led by Nikolai Brusentsov, with the support of mathematician Sergei Sobolev, they used balanced ternary: −1, 0 and +1 instead of binary’s two digits.

The machine was called Setun, after a nearby river. Ferrite cores and semiconductor diodes formed its logic. Three-valued arithmetic gave the designers a compact way to handle positive and negative numbers. This was a working computer, not just a mathematical proposal.

Fifty machines were produced, including the prototypes. Setun reached universities and research institutes, but ternary computing never became the industry’s mainstream. Brusentsov’s team later developed Setun 70.

Its place in this drawer is not a claim that three always beats two. It is a reminder that the familiar architecture was not the only architecture engineers could build.

Why it’s here: A working alternative can disappear without ever being disproved.

UG / 002 · COMPUTING1936

The Water Computer

ГИДРАВЛИЧЕСКИЙ ИНТЕГРАТОР

Inventor:
Vladimir Lukyanov
Medium:
Water, tubes, and controlled flow
Filed under:
Analogue computing / heat transfer

The answer is
in the water level.

Read story ЧИТАТЬ

A computer does not have to look like a box of electronics. In 1936, Vladimir Lukyanov built a hydraulic integrator: a network of water-filled vessels and connecting tubes that could model problems in heat flow.

The trick was an analogy. Water moving through a restricted pipe could represent heat passing through a material. Vessel size represented thermal capacity; water level represented the changing quantity being calculated. Set up the apparatus correctly, and the water’s behavior supplied the solution.

This was analogue computation, not a digital machine secretly running underwater. It was built for a particular family of mathematical problems, including the behavior of heat in construction materials.

About 150 hydraulic integrators were produced in the Soviet Union, according to the historical survey linked below. Some were exported. The glass tubes were not a joke or a failed sketch: they were useful computing equipment before electronic methods took over much of that work.

Why it’s here: Sometimes the material itself can do the calculating.

UG / 003 · RADIO & SOUND1945

“The Thing”

ЛЕВ ТЕРМЕН · ПАССИВНЫЙ РЕЗОНАТОР

Inventor:
Lev Termen / Léon Theremin
Device:
The Great Seal listening device
Filed under:
Radio / acoustic resonance

No battery.
Still listening.

Read story ЧИТАТЬ

In August 1945, Soviet schoolchildren presented U.S. Ambassador Averell Harriman with a carved wooden Great Seal of the United States. It was hung in his Moscow residential office. Hidden inside was a listening device associated with Lev Termen, better known as Léon Theremin.

The device needed no battery. An outside radio beam supplied the energy. Voices in the room moved a thin membrane inside a resonant cavity, changing the reflected radio signal. The listeners outside could recover the sound.

When the illuminating beam was off, the bug was silent. That made it unusually difficult to find with methods looking for an active transmitter. The device was discovered in 1952, years after the gift arrived.

American technicians called it “The Thing.” The ingenuity deserves study; the intrusion does not need celebration. A wooden emblem had concealed a remarkably economical piece of radio engineering.

Why it’s here: A tiny passive resonator turned an apparently harmless object into an ear.

UG / 004 · STORY 011903

The Deaf Schoolteacher Who Did the Math for Space

КОНСТАНТИН ЦИОЛКОВСКИЙ

In 1903, a provincial teacher with no university degree wrote the equation every rocket still obeys.

Read story ЧИТАТЬ

Konstantin Tsiolkovsky lost most of his hearing to scarlet fever as a child and taught himself, spending his teenage years in Moscow's libraries. He became a schoolteacher in Kaluga, and in his spare time he worked out how a rocket could reach space. His 1903 paper Exploration of Outer Space by Means of Reaction Devices contained the rocket equation, which links a rocket's change in speed to its exhaust speed and to how much of its mass is fuel. He also sketched liquid-fuel rockets, multistage rockets, airlocks and orbiting stations. His work reached a much wider audience in the 1920s, and the Soviet space program later treated him as its founding father.

Why it’s here: The math was right decades before anyone could build the machine.

UG / 005 · STORY 021916–1929

The Man Who Sketched a Route to the Moon

ЮРИЙ КОНДРАТЮК · АЛЕКСАНДР ШАРГЕЙ

Decades before Apollo, a young theorist described a small lander meeting its mothership in lunar orbit.

Read story ЧИТАТЬ

Born Aleksandr Shargei, he filled notebooks with rocket calculations during World War I, entirely on his own. One of his key ideas: don't land the whole spacecraft on the Moon. Park the main ship in lunar orbit, and send a small lander down and back up. That is lunar orbit rendezvous, the plan Apollo used. He published it in 1929, in a book he paid to print himself. Having served in the White Army, he lived on the papers of a dead man named Yuri Kondratyuk to avoid arrest. He spent his career designing grain elevators and wind power plants, and died at the front in World War II. NASA engineers later developed and adopted lunar orbit rendezvous for Apollo. Kondratyuk was an important early proposer; this does not establish that Apollo was copied directly from his book.

Why it’s here: His idea flew to the Moon. He never saw a rocket launch.

UG / 006 · STORY 031910–1912

The Actor Who Invented the Backpack Parachute

ГЛЕБ КОТЕЛЬНИКОВ

A theater actor watched a pilot fall to his death and decided to fix the problem himself.

Read story ЧИТАТЬ

In 1910, Gleb Kotelnikov saw the aviator Lev Matsievich fall from his plane at an air show in St. Petersburg. A practical escape system for pilots was still an urgent problem. Kotelnikov designed a compact one packed into a backpack and opened with a ripcord: the RK-1. To test it, he tied the parachute to a moving car and pulled the cord, and the car was dragged to a stop. The test also demonstrated a use for parachutes as brakes. Russian officials weren't interested at first, so he patented it in France. His RK-1 became an important early backpack parachute design.

Why it’s here: A man with no engineering training solved what the aviators hadn't.

UG / 007 · STORY 041907–1911

The Father of Electronic TV Who Died in Exile

БОРИС РОЗИНГ

In 1911, Boris Rosing demonstrated an early television system with an electronic receiving screen.

Read story ЧИТАТЬ

Physics professor Boris Rosing realized that a cathode ray tube could draw pictures. He patented an electronic receiver in 1907, and in 1911 he transmitted simple geometric shapes onto a tube screen. The camera side was still mechanical, but the screen was electronic, a step toward later electronic television. His student Vladimir Zworykin emigrated to the United States and became one of the main inventors of American television at RCA. Rosing stayed. In 1931 he was arrested in a political case and exiled to Arkhangelsk, where he died in 1933.

Why it’s here: His student built an industry. He got exile.

UG / 008 · STORY 051903

The Man Named "Color" Who Separated Colors

МИХАИЛ ЦВЕТ

Drug tests, crime labs and food labs all use an invention by a Russian botanist almost nobody remembers.

Read story ЧИТАТЬ

In work he presented in 1903 and described more fully in 1906, Mikhail Tsvet poured a leaf extract through a glass tube packed with chalk powder. As it moved down, the green liquid split into separate colored bands: the different chlorophylls and carotenoids, pulled apart. He named the method chromatography, "color writing." His surname, Tsvet, means "color" in Russian. Leading chemists dismissed the work, and it sat mostly ignored. Tsvet died in 1919, during the Civil War. Chromatography was rediscovered in the 1930s and is now one of the most used techniques in chemistry, the basis of a Nobel Prize and of countless lab instruments.

Why it’s here: The idea was 30 years early.

UG / 009 · STORY 061905–1915

Color Photos of a Lost Empire

СЕРГЕЙ ПРОКУДИН-ГОРСКИЙ

Vivid color photographs of the Russian Empire, taken more than 100 years ago, sat in boxes for decades.

Read story ЧИТАТЬ

Between about 1905 and 1915, chemist and photographer Sergei Prokudin-Gorsky traveled the empire with the backing of Tsar Nicholas II, in a railway car fitted out as a darkroom. His method: three quick black-and-white exposures of the same scene, through red, green and blue filters. Projected together through matching colored light, they made a full-color image. He photographed peasants, monasteries, factories, the Emir of Bukhara, and a well-known 1908 portrait of Leo Tolstoy. After the revolution he left Russia with many of his glass plates. The US Library of Congress bought them from his family in 1948. The pictures could be projected in color in his own time. Digital reconstruction in the early 2000s made the collection much easier for a worldwide audience to see.

Why it’s here: He recorded an empire in color; digital reconstruction brought his archive to a much wider public.

UG / 010 · STORY 071913–1939

The Airliner Russia Lost

ИГОРЬ СИКОРСКИЙ

In 1913, Igor Sikorsky built pioneering four-engine aircraft. He later started over in America.

Read story ЧИТАТЬ

In Kyiv and St. Petersburg, the young Igor Sikorsky built the Russky Vityaz and then the Ilya Muromets: huge for its time, with four engines, a heated passenger cabin, and even a lavatory. In 1914 it flew from St. Petersburg to Kyiv and back. During World War I the design was developed into an early four-engine heavy bomber. After the revolution, Sikorsky emigrated and arrived in New York in 1919 with very little money. He started over: he taught, built flying boats, and in 1939 flew the VS-300, which set the layout of the modern helicopter, one main rotor plus a small tail rotor.

Why it’s here: One of aviation's greatest talents, raised in one country, did his life's work in another.

The Portable Radiophone of 1957

ЛЕОНИД КУПРИЯНОВИЧ · ЛК-1

A Moscow engineer demonstrated a portable radiophone years before handheld cellular phones.

Read story ЧИТАТЬ

In 1957, Leonid Kupriyanovich described the LK-1 in the Soviet magazine Наука и жизнь. A portable radio unit communicated through a fixed station connected to the telephone network. The first version weighed about 3 kg; a smaller model was reported in 1958. These were experimental radiophones, rather than a demonstrated modern cellular network with automatic handover between cells. Contemporary reports show an ambitious attempt to take telephone calls away from the wire. A direct technical lineage to the later Altai car-phone system is not established here.

The Transplant Pioneer Who Struggled for Support

ВЛАДИМИР ДЕМИХОВ

Before the first human heart transplant, the surgeon who did it visited a Soviet scientist many colleagues dismissed.

Read story ЧИТАТЬ

Vladimir Demikhov was a biologist, not a licensed surgeon, working with dogs in Moscow from the 1940s. He performed pioneering heart and heart-lung transplantation experiments in animals and developed coronary bypass techniques. In 1954 he became infamous for grafting the head and front legs of a puppy onto a larger dog. The photos went around the world and made him look like a mad scientist. At home he was attacked as unscientific, and his lab was often starved of support. Surgeons abroad took him seriously: Christiaan Barnard visited him in the 1960s, before performing the first human heart transplant in 1967.

Why it’s here: His methods saved lives elsewhere while his own career stalled.

UG / 013 · STORY 101942

The Movie Star's Patent the Navy Shelved

ХЕДИ ЛАМАРР · ДЖОРДЖ АНТЕЙЛ

A Hollywood actress co-invented the idea behind jam-proof wireless, and the US Navy filed it away.

Read story ЧИТАТЬ

During World War II, actress Hedy Lamarr and composer George Antheil designed a way to stop radio-guided torpedoes from being jammed: the transmitter and receiver hop between frequencies in the same secret pattern. Their synchronization used punched rolls like a player piano's, across 88 frequencies, the number of keys on a piano. They were granted a patent in 1942 and offered it to the Navy, which shelved it. Frequency hopping later became a building block of military radio, and it is used in Bluetooth today. Lamarr was recognized only late in life, with an Electronic Frontier Foundation award in 1997.

Why it’s here: The world saw a movie star, not an inventor.

UG / 014 · STORY 111940–1944

The Seed Keepers of Leningrad

НИКОЛАЙ ВАВИЛОВ · ХРАНИТЕЛИ СЕМЯН

During the Siege of Leningrad, scientists starved to death while guarding rooms full of edible seeds.

Read story ЧИТАТЬ

Nikolai Vavilov built the world's largest seed collection at his institute in Leningrad, gathered on expeditions across five continents to preserve the genetic roots of the world's food crops. In 1940 he was arrested during the political campaign against genetics, and in 1943 he died in prison of starvation. Meanwhile, through the 872-day siege, his staff hid and guarded the collection: wheat, rice, potatoes, enough to eat. They didn't touch it. Several of them died of hunger at their posts, surrounded by food, because the seeds were meant to feed the future. The collection survived and is still kept at the institute that now carries Vavilov's name.

Why it’s here: The man who wanted to end famine died of hunger, and so did the people who saved his work.

UG / 015 · STORY 121950s

The Kurgan Doctor Who Grew Bones

ГАВРИИЛ ИЛИЗАРОВ

A provincial surgeon in Kurgan built a device that can grow new bone and lengthen legs.

Read story ЧИТАТЬ

In the 1950s, Gavriil Ilizarov was treating World War II veterans whose fractures wouldn't heal, in Kurgan, far from the big medical centers. He built an external frame of steel rings joined by rods, with thin tensioned wires through the bone, like the spokes of a bicycle wheel. Then he found something nobody expected: if you slowly pull the two halves of a bone apart, under carefully controlled gradual distraction, new bone grows in the gap. The Moscow medical establishment ignored him for years. Fame came through his patients: Olympic high jumper Valery Brumel, whose shattered leg he saved, and the Italian explorer Carlo Mauri in 1980. Italian surgeons then brought the method West, and the Ilizarov apparatus is now used around the world.

Why it’s here: Genius in a provincial city had to be discovered by foreigners.

UG / 016 · STORY 13late 1940s–1960s

Music on Ribs

МУЗЫКА НА РЁБРАХ

Soviet music lovers bootlegged jazz and rock'n'roll onto used X-ray films.

Read story ЧИТАТЬ

From the late 1940s into the 1960s, Western jazz and rock, along with banned émigré singers, were hard to get legally in the USSR. Vinyl was controlled, but hospitals threw out old X-ray film. Underground recordists cut the grooves into the film with improvised recording machines, trimmed it into a disc with scissors, and burned the center hole with a cigarette. Hold one up to the light and you could see someone's ribs or skull while the music played. The sound was rough, and a disc lasted only a few plays. People called them "bones" or "ribs."

Why it’s here: A whole black-market music industry, built on hospital trash.

UG / 017 · STORY 141984

The Hit Its Inventor Didn't Own

АЛЕКСЕЙ ПАЖИТНОВ · ТЕТРИС

One of the best-selling games ever was written on a Soviet lab computer, and its creator earned nothing from it for over a decade.

Read story ЧИТАТЬ

In 1984, Alexey Pajitnov was a researcher at the Academy of Sciences' computing center in Moscow, testing an Elektronika 60 computer that could show only text. He made a game of falling shapes, each built from four squares and drawn with brackets. The name came from tetromino plus tennis, his favorite sport. Copies spread across Moscow on floppy disks, then slipped through the Iron Curtain. Because he worked for the state, the state owned the rights, and Western companies fought a tangled battle over the licenses. Pajitnov started earning royalties only after he co-founded The Tetris Company in 1996.

Why it’s here: Made for fun, owned by the state.

UG / 018 · STORY 151950s–1969

The Soviet Machine the World Copied

ТОКАМАК · ТАММ · САХАРОВ

The machine at the heart of the world's biggest fusion project has a Russian name.

He stayed blunt and stubborn all his life, famously confident in his own judgment, and led his bureau until his death in 1972. His son Alexei continued his work, including the Tu-144.

Read story ЧИТАТЬ

Nikolai Kozyrev developed what he called "causal mechanics": the idea that time is not just a measure of change but a physical thing that flows, has a direction, and can transfer energy. His proposed energy-bearing properties of time have not been established by reproducible evidence and are not accepted physical theory. But Kozyrev was also a careful observer. In 1958, at the Crimean Observatory, he recorded a spectrum from the lunar crater Alphonsus that he interpreted as evidence of gas emission. The person behind it ЧЕЛОВЕК ЗА ИДЕЕЙ

Kozyrev was born in 1908 in St. Petersburg and was a star young astrophysicist at the Pulkovo Observatory. In 1936 he was arrested in a wave of repression against Pulkovo astronomers and sentenced to ten years. He spent them in camps and prisons in the far north, including Norilsk, doing hard labor. He later said that in prison, with no instruments, he kept thinking about why stars shine and what time really is. He was released in 1946 and eventually allowed back to science. He spent the rest of his life defending ideas that colleagues found strange, and died in 1983.

Why it’s here: A persecuted astronomer pursued an extraordinary idea; the human history is documented, while the claimed physics remains unverified.

He was famously honest and short-tempered with incompetent officials and corrupt suppliers, which made him powerful enemies. After the war he turned to education reform and was pushed out of government posts for his liberal views. He spent his last years on his estate near Vinnytsia, treating local peasants for free. He died in 1881. His body was embalmed and is still kept in a crypt there.

Why it’s here: Anatomical mapping, battlefield anesthesia and organized wartime care met in one surgeon’s work.

Lunin was born in 1853 in Dorpat in the Russian Empire. After his mouse experiments he didn't stay in research. He became a children's doctor in St. Petersburg and spent decades treating sick children, never trying to claim credit for his early discovery. He died in 1937. Only later did historians of science recognize that his simple experiment had pointed straight at vitamins.

Why it’s here: His feeding experiments pointed toward missing essential nutrients, decades before individual vitamins were isolated.

In 1935 a man named Charles Darrow sold one of those versions to Parker Brothers as Monopoly, presented as his own invention. Parker Brothers bought Magie's patent for $500.

The person behind it ЧЕЛОВЕК ЗА ИДЕЕЙ

Magie was born in 1866 in Illinois. She worked as a stenographer and typist and also wrote poetry and acted on stage. She was outspoken about women's economic dependence: in 1906 she placed a mock newspaper ad offering herself for sale as a "young woman American slave" to protest how few choices women had. For decades Darrow was celebrated as Monopoly's inventor. She died in 1948, and her role was rediscovered only in the 1970s.

Why it’s here: Her anti-monopoly game became the world's most famous celebration of monopoly, under someone else's name.

Chester Carlson worked in a patent office in New York, where documents had to be copied by hand or photographed at great expense. He wanted a dry, fast way to copy using light and static electricity. He experimented at home, filling his kitchen with smoke and fumes, then rented a small room in Astoria, Queens. The person behind it ЧЕЛОВЕК ЗА ИДЕЕЙ

Carlson was born in 1906 in Seattle into a poor family. Both his parents were ill, and from his early teens he worked to support them; his mother died when he was 17. He studied physics while working, went to law school at night, and suffered from arthritis that made the hand-copying in his job painful. Xerography made him very rich, and he quietly gave most of his fortune away to charities, often anonymously. He died in 1968.

Why it’s here: A document-copying problem led from home experiments to a laboratory breakthrough and a major office technology.

Josephine Cochrane designed wire compartments shaped to hold plates, cups and saucers, fitted inside a wheel in a copper boiler. As the wheel turned, hot soapy water sprayed up onto the dishes. She patented it in 1886, and at the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago her machines won an award and were used in restaurants at the fair. Hotels and restaurants bought them first. The person behind it ЧЕЛОВЕК ЗА ИДЕЕЙ

Cochrane was born in 1839 in Ohio; her great-grandfather, John Fitch, had built one of America's first steamboats. When her husband died in 1883, he left her with large debts. Instead of selling her house, she turned the dishwasher into a business, building the first machines in a shed behind her house with the help of a mechanic, George Butters. She sold them herself, at a time when women rarely ran manufacturing businesses, and ran the company until her death in 1913.

Why it’s here: A widow in debt turned an annoyance into an industry.

As a child working in a cotton mill in New Hampshire, Margaret Knight saw a steel-tipped shuttle fly out of a loom and injure a worker. Later, working at a paper bag company, she invented a machine that folded and glued flat-bottomed paper bags, the kind still used in grocery stores today. While she was having an iron model made, a man named Charles Annan saw it, copied it and patented it first. She sued. Annan argued that a woman could not possibly have designed such a machine. Knight brought her notebooks, drawings and witnesses, and in 1871 she won her patent.

The person behind it ЧЕЛОВЕК ЗА ИДЕЕЙ

Knight was born in 1838 in Maine. Her father died when she was a child, and she went to work in the mill to help her family. She never had formal engineering training, but she loved tools and was building things from childhood. Over her life she had around 90 inventions and more than 20 patents, including parts for shoe-cutting machines and engines. The press called her a "woman Edison." She never married and died in 1914 with little money.

Why it’s here: A mill girl with no training won in court against a man who said women can't invent.

The Candy Bar That Melted in His Pocket

In 1945, Percy Spencer at Raytheon was working near a magnetron, the tube that produced microwaves for radar, when he noticed the candy bar in his pocket had melted. Raytheon patented microwave cooking, and in 1947 it sold the first microwave oven, the "Radarange," which stood almost as tall as a man and weighed over 300 kg. It took decades of development before microwave ovens became small and cheap enough for ordinary kitchens.

The person behind it ЧЕЛОВЕК ЗА ИДЕЕЙ

Spencer was born in 1894 in Maine. His father died when he was a baby, and his mother left, so he was raised by an aunt and uncle. He left school at 12 to work in a spool mill, and taught himself electricity, then radio in the US Navy. At Raytheon during World War II, he found a way to mass-produce magnetrons for Allied radar, raising production from a handful a day to thousands. For the microwave oven, he received the company's standard bonus for a patent, reportedly about two dollars. He rose to senior vice president and died in 1970.

Why it’s here: A boy from a spool mill noticed what the experts missed, in his own pocket.
